# The Search - Episode Guide

This document describes what is supposed to happen in each episode: the story
the audience watches, the decisions production makes, and the consequences that
carry into the next round.

Copied episode Markdown presents The Search as a televised competition to find
the next Squirrel Zone, never as a simulation. Include the closing investigation
only when that episode actually reveals a secret; omit unsuccessful or skipped
checks, their shortlist scenes and investigation teasers. Actual revelations
remain part of the story. These copy rules do not change episode playback.

**Copy Show So Far**, beside **Clear Simulation**, copies one Markdown document:
the show's introduction, all season-entry contestant profiles, then completed
episode rundowns in chronological order. It also works before the first episode,
when only the introduction and cast are available. Unfinished episodes are omitted.

## After each episode: Relationships Updates

The **Relationships Updates** button after every finale opens a separate,
viewer-only screen. These are observations about the episode, not a broadcast
sequence, host announcement or additional interaction.

Highlight every directed relationship whose net change is greater than five
points in either direction, compared with the previous episode's ending.
Auditions use season-entry relationships as their baseline. Show the direction,
size of the change and final value, not every intermediate swing. Changed
relationships can include contestants who departed.

Also highlight mutual-feeling gaps of at least fifteen points among that
episode's cast, including contestants who leave during the episode. List each
pair once and show both directed values; do not imply that feelings are mutual.
Keep earlier summaries anchored to their own episode's saved history.

Copied episode rundowns and **Copy Show So Far** include the same notes in a
clearly labeled, non-broadcast section. Viewing or copying them never changes
relationships, results, TV score, experience or the saved episode.

## Episode 1: Auditions

Fifty contestants get their first chance to show the public who they are. They
are competing for one of the five places in the next iteration of the pop
sensation **Squirrel Zone**, but tonight is only the first cut. By the end of
Auditions, twenty contestants will leave and thirty will advance.

The episode should feel like a televised introduction to the cast, not a parade
of scorecards. Some contestants receive a complete introduction and performance;
others appear in brisk montages. Everyone still auditions, receives an
evaluation, makes an impression on the public, and is watched by the other
contestants.

### Before the episode: production's choices

The episode pauses for two decisions before Mike Philip welcomes the audience.

#### Choose the opening cast

The player selects **five contestants from a shortlist of ten** to appear in the
opening sequence.

The shortlist favors contestants with the strongest average across the seven
core skills: vocals, rap, dance, stage presence, visual, uniqueness, and
leadership. A better visual skill breaks an equal average; otherwise, a random
draw settles the tie.

These five opening slots become a continuing part of the show's identity. In
later episodes, surviving opening contestants keep their places, and production
replaces only those who have been eliminated. Being selected for the opening
does not guarantee survival or a featured audition.

#### Choose the opening mood

The player chooses one of **two distinct options in each of six categories**:
vocal color, dance style, rap style, visual vibe, leadership style, and genre
affinity.

Together, these choices establish the episode's audience tastes and spotlight.
They influence public appeal, not the judges' personal criteria or the scoring
weights.

**Randomize** fills the six selections for the player, but the player must still
confirm the mood. The episode does not begin until both production decisions
have been confirmed.

#### What contestants decide for themselves

Contestants choose their own audition songs from a shared pool of nine songs:
three vocal, three rap, and three dance arrangements, covering different levels
of difficulty. Skill fit, genre affinity, and personality influence their
choices.

Most remain in their usual track. Some take a risk and choose another track,
while especially disciplined, mature, or invested contestants avoid that risk.
The player does not choose songs for them.

There is no advance song-board reveal. A contestant's song, and any decision to
go off-track, becomes part of the story only when that contestant is introduced
for their audition.

The audition order is random. Position determines who receives a featured
audition or montage appearance; production does not assign that coverage after
seeing who performed well.

the final auditions and the results, without a separate vote-summary sequence.

### Sequence 1: Mike welcomes the audience

**Mike Philip**, the host, establishes the stakes: fifty contestants, five
eventual places in Squirrel Zone, and individual auditions before a panel of
judges. Only thirty contestants will reach the next round.

His welcome should invite the audience into the show and make tonight's task
clear. This is an introduction to the competition, not an announcement of any
contestant's prospects or results.

Mike speaks directly to the audience in a named speech bubble. The player
continues when ready to watch the opening.

### Sequence 2: The opening sequence

The five contestants chosen by production appear in an opening built around
their portraits and names. It should feel like the title sequence of the show,
giving those faces a little extra familiarity before the auditions begin.

Each opening contestant receives a small popularity benefit: **+0.02 audience
preference**, capped at the maximum preference of +1. This does not directly
award fans, judge points, or protection from elimination.

The benefit happens once for this episode. Watching the opening again does not
keep increasing anyone's popularity. The opening's attention is separate from
the screen time given to the auditions.

### Sequence 3: Meet the judges

Mike introduces three people with different ideas about what makes a future
member of Squirrel Zone:

| Judge            | Background                  | What matters to them                                                                   |
| ---------------- | --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **John Stan**    | Male music producer         | Technical performance: the vocal, rap, and dance skills actually demanded by the song. |
| **Megan Ryder**  | Female veteran artist       | Star quality: stage presence, potential, and uniqueness, considered equally.           |
| **Michael Lion** | Male record-label executive | Marketability: visual, consistency, and leadership, considered equally.                |

The judges keep their individual priorities throughout the episode. What
changes is **whose opinion carries extra weight**. Every seven auditions, the
leading judge passes from John to Megan to Michael, then back to John.

Mike also explains that doing the song well is essential regardless of who is
leading the panel. The song's track-skill evaluation counts three times, the
leading judge's evaluation counts twice, and each other judge counts once.

### How every contestant is introduced

All fifty contestants receive a first introduction, including the five already
seen in the opening.

The introduction gives their name, usual track, zodiac sign, persona, public
identity and interests, and specialty highlights. Personas, identities, and
interests are described through their **card descriptions**, with the card's
actual name appearing as a tag afterward. The chosen track is also tagged.

For example, an introduction can read:

> Declan steps forward: he chose the **Dance** track, his zodiac sign is Scorpio.
> Even when he's silent, drama finds him. If there is a fight in the kitchen,
> he's somehow in the middle of it. Editors love his reaction shots.
> **The Drama Magnet**

Here, **Dance** and **The Drama Magnet** represent the on-screen tags, rather
than additional headings or a list of unexplained card titles.

Especially pronounced personality traits are highlighted: values above +5 or
below -5. A value of exactly +5 or -5 is not treated as an extreme introduction
highlight.

The introduction then reveals the song. If the contestant is stepping outside
their usual track, this is the moment to call attention to the gamble. Hidden
secrets are not included in the introduction.

### Sequence 4: The first three featured auditions

The first three contestants receive full attention. Each audition unfolds in
**two separate moments**:
1. The contestant is introduced, giving the audience a person to recognize
   before they hear a result.
2. The player continues to the performance, grade, leading judge's comment, and
   a few significant reactions.

John is the leading judge for all three auditions. His comments should draw on
what the contestant actually demonstrated, recognizing technical strengths and
weaknesses rather than inventing mishaps or accomplishments.

These first performances also establish the backstage atmosphere. Contestants
begin comparing themselves with the people they will have to compete against.

### Sequence 5: Twelve auditions in a montage

Audition positions **4-15** become a fast-moving reel of new faces, song choices,
and results. Each contestant gets a brief introduction and grade, with fuller
public introductions available to expand.

This is one condensed sequence, not twelve individually advanced featured
auditions. The lighter presentation does not mean anyone escapes evaluation or
has their consequences skipped.

The panel's emphasis changes inside the montage: John leads through audition 7,
Megan takes over at audition 8, and Michael becomes the lead at audition 15.

### Sequence 6: Five featured auditions

Positions **16-20** return to the slower introduction-then-performance format.
Each contestant has room to establish their identity and receive an individual
judge comment.

Michael leads throughout this block. His perspective brings visual appeal,
consistency, and leadership into focus, while the song's track-skill check
remains the largest single part of every result.

### Sequence 7: Ten auditions in a montage

Positions **21-30** appear together in the second montage. The audience meets
another ten contestants through short introductions and results, with details
available without turning the reel into a long sequence of score tables.

Michael leads audition 21. John takes over at 22, and Megan takes over at 29.
Earlier performances and backstage reactions can still affect contestants who
have yet to perform.

### Sequence 8: Five featured auditions

Positions **31-35** receive full introductions and individual performance beats.
Megan leads all five, placing special emphasis on stage presence, potential, and
uniqueness.

This block should restore the feeling of spending time with individual people
after the montage. Grades matter, but so do the impressions contestants leave
on their peers and the public.

### Sequence 9: Twelve auditions in a montage

Positions **36-47** form the final montage. Twelve more contestants receive
their first introductions and audition results, bringing the cast closer to
being fully known.

Michael leads auditions 36-42, and John takes over at 43. All twelve performances
still affect opinions, readiness, audience reception, and the eventual cut.

### Sequence 10: The final three featured auditions

Positions **48-50** close the auditions with full attention. Each has a separate
introduction, performance, grade, judge comment, and highlighted reactions.

John leads auditions 48 and 49. Megan takes over for audition 50: the rotation
counts actual contestants, so the final audition begins a new seven-person
block even though no more auditions follow.

By the end of this sequence, all fifty contestants have performed. Sixteen have
received featured auditions, and thirty-four have appeared in the three
montages.

### What happens throughout the auditions

#### Performance evaluation

Every audition combines four evaluations in a **3:2:1:1 balance**:

| Contribution                                                                 | Weight |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------ |
| The chosen song's track checked against the contestant's corresponding skill | 3      |
| The leading judge's evaluation                                               | 2      |
| The second judge's evaluation                                                | 1      |
| The third judge's evaluation                                                 | 1      |

These seven weighted parts produce the raw panel score. A vocal song tests vocals,
a rap song tests rap, and a dance song tests dance. Going off-track does not
allow a contestant to fall back on their usual specialty.

The song's difficulty matters, as does execution on the day. John also considers
the other technical skills an arrangement actually calls for, giving its primary
skill extra emphasis within his evaluation. Unused technical skills are not
invented as additional requirements.

Newly started current-format Auditions receive a gentler final evaluation:
**final = raw panel score + 20% of its remaining distance to 100**. For example,
50 becomes 60, 65 becomes 72, and 80 becomes 84. Only the final result changes;
the recorded dice, technical checks and individual judges' evaluations do not.
The raw result and adjustment are available in optional Details. Already-started
Auditions retain their original scale throughout, including after reloading.
Legacy-format seasons and later episodes' scoring rules remain unchanged.

The grade bands are **A at 85 or above, B at 70, C at 55, D at 40, and F below
40**. Detailed checks remain available for inspection, but the episode itself
foregrounds the performance, grade, and judge's response.

#### Opinions, reactions, and composure

Every contestant forms a small opinion of every other contestant's audition,
even without a direct conversation. These opinions are directional: one
contestant's admiration does not automatically mean the feeling is mutual.
Nobody forms a relationship with themselves.

Some performances provoke a stronger response: admiration, inspiration,
intimidation, jealousy, confidence, or sympathy. Personality and comparisons
with the performer shape these reactions. They can create larger relationship
changes than the quiet first impressions formed by everyone else.

Watching others may also change **composure**, the temporary readiness a
contestant brings to their own performance. It can help or hinder stage presence
without permanently changing skills or altering the technical song check.
There are no rehearsal or practice sessions in this episode.

The audience sees only notable reaction highlights, usually no more than two
per audition scene or montage. They appear as secondary notes beneath the main
story rather than overwhelming the performance. Other opinions and changes
still count, even when they are not narrated.

#### Screen time and public support

Each featured audition receives a full share of attention. Each entire montage
receives one comparable share, divided equally among its contestants. Across
the episode, that means sixteen individual shares and three shared montage
shares.

All fifty contestants receive some coverage, but the featured contestants have
more opportunity to become familiar to the public. Leaving a scene open longer
or replaying it does not create more screen time.

Public reception reflects the performances, the episode's mood, existing
preferences, and reaction footage actually shown. Contestants can gain fans,
and audience enjoyment influences the show's continuing health.

After the final audition, voting closes without interrupting the story for a
production choice or a separate voting screen. **There is no broadcast-package
selection in Auditions.**

Public support contributes to the continuing overall ranking: 60% performance,
30% normalized votes, and 10% production preference, with 75% of a previous
cumulative score retained when applicable. This is separate from tonight's
elimination decision. The formal combined-ranking announcement is held for
Episode 2.

### Sequence 12: Twenty goodbyes

Mike returns to announce the results. The twenty weakest audition evaluations
leave; the other thirty contestants advance. **Public votes cannot save a
contestant from this cut.**

The departures are revealed in **four groups of five**, with a pause between
announcements. The scene should make room for disappointment, relief, sadness,
and the joy of still having a chance, rather than treating departures as a
silent change to a roster.

After each group, the thirty eventual survivors react to the people just
announced:
- A strong positive connection, measured at 53 or above when results began,
  grows by 2 as the survivor feels the loss of that contestant.
- A strong negative connection, measured at 47 or below, drops by 2 as the
  survivor feels relief at the departure.
- Neutral connections do not change.

Each change is the survivor's feeling **toward the departing contestant**.
It happens once, stays within the relationship scale of 0-100, and does not
automatically change the departed contestant's feelings in return.

These reactions use the connections that existed before the announcements, so
one farewell does not change who qualifies to react to a later group. There is
no automatic resentment toward unrelated survivors for someone else's
elimination.

Only a couple of genuine emotional highlights accompany each group. A reaction
can hint that its speaker will survive, but the episode does not formally label
them a survivor ahead of the results.

After all twenty departures, thirty contestants remain. Some fans of eliminated
contestants find someone else to support; others return to being general
viewers.

### Closing coda: The survivors' MVP

The thirty survivors privately vote for an MVP from among themselves. Nobody
can vote for themselves.

Their choices reflect the performances they watched, their relationships, and
personality affinity. This is peer recognition, not another public vote or a
decision made by the player. A tie for the most votes is settled by a random
draw.

The winner receives a dedicated announcement before the next production
decision appears. The honor is remembered, but it grants no hidden ranking
points and does not reopen the elimination results.

### Closing coda: A secret investigation

Mike introduces one last production decision. The player must choose **one of
three shortlisted contestants** to investigate before the episode can close.
The shortlist can include someone who has just been eliminated.

A choice is not a guarantee that a secret exists. If the investigation finds
nothing, the episode acknowledges that and nothing changes.

If a secret is uncovered, its name and description become public, and its
consequences matter. Changes that can take effect without overturning the
episode happen now; any consequence that would rewrite a locked elimination or
ranking result is held for later.

The decision is presented in a blocking modal. The episode remains paused until
the player's choice is confirmed successfully.

### End of episode

The episode closes with **thirty survivors and twenty departures**. Performances,
grades, public support, relationships, farewells, MVP recognition, and any secret
reveal remain part of the contestants' continuing story.

Episode 2 does not start automatically. The next chapter will bring the thirty
survivors to their dorms and then to the stage for captain selection and team
battles. The first full survivor ranking follows at the end of Episode 2.

### Presentation and player control

The player advances the episode deliberately, one story beat at a time. Featured
introductions and performances are separate beats; a montage is a single
condensed beat. The show never rushes through an unresolved player decision.

Mike's dialogue and the judges' comments have named speech bubbles. Contestant
names in ordinary prose open their details, with portrait buttons after smaller
sections. Large cast lists and rankings use portraits directly rather than
adding another long row of buttons.

Grades, composure, relationships, tracks, and introduction card names use tags.
Increases have a green upward arrow and decreases have a red downward arrow,
showing the new value rather than repeating the old one.

## Episode 2: The Battles

Thirty contestants arrive in a new home. Individual auditions have shown who
can stand alone; now they must learn to lead, cooperate, and trust teammates.
Six teams will face three battles, and only eighteen contestants will remain.
The three saves are a surprise revealed late in the episode, not part of Mike's
initial explanation.

### Sequence overview

| Sequence | What the audience sees                                                                                                                          |
| -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1        | Five groups of six arrive at their assigned dorm rooms, with first impressions and roommate interactions.                                       |
| 2        | The five-person opening sequence plays and its small popularity benefit takes effect.                                                           |
| 3        | Mike explains the challenge, announces six captains, follows every draft pick, and introduces the three matchups.                               |
| 4        | The first pair's rehearsals, leadership, center votes, production decisions, and performances.                                                  |
| 5        | The same full treatment for the second pair of teams.                                                                                           |
| 6        | The final pair performs; audience voting closes quietly afterward.                                                                              |
| 7        | All three judges review every team, then Mike announces the winners and provisional eliminations.                                               |
| 8        | Three saves, final goodbyes, peer MVP, a secret investigation, Mike's new elimination rule, and the first full survivor ranking with reactions. |

### Before the episode

The player chooses this episode's opening mood. Each specialty offers two
options based on the previous standings' highest- and lowest-ranked surviving
contestants, without identifying whose preferences they are. Randomize fills
the choices but still requires confirmation.

Surviving members of the opening cast keep their slots. If anyone has left,
production offers a strong-core-skill shortlist from which the player fills
only the empty places.

### Sequence 1: A new home

Production randomly divides the thirty contestants into **five rooms of six**.
There is no room selection or chance to reroll the assignments.

The episode follows each group arriving. All six contestants have a first
impression of their roommates: a familiar face may be reassuring, an awkward
connection may feel uncomfortable, and strangers may inspire curiosity or
guardedness.

Small interactions reveal harmony or tension. A real conversation can improve
or damage a bond; merely sleeping in the same room does not automatically make
everyone friends. Only notable relationship consequences become reaction notes.

Nobody sees the complete ranking in the dorms.

### Sequence 2: Opening credits

Play the five-person opening sequence. Each featured contestant receives the
same small, once-per-episode popularity benefit as in Auditions. It does not
guarantee a battle win or award judge points.

The story moves from the dorms to the stage the next morning.

### Sequence 3: Captains, teams, and opponents

Mike Philip explains that the contestants will form **six teams of five**.
Each team must prepare a group performance and defeat an opposing team.

The six highest contestants in the previous combined standings become captains.
Mike announces those six in order, but does not reveal the full thirty-person
ranking. The complete survivor ranking is reserved for the finale.

Each team takes its captain's name: **Team Justin**, for example.

#### Choosing teammates

Captains take turns in order, one through six, for four rounds. Each pick gets
its own moment, including why the captain chose that person.

After each pick, "Now the team consists of..." lists the captain and everyone
selected so far. The names open contestant details. If the new teammate has a
strained relationship with someone already on the team, a brief reaction
highlights the discomfort. One-sided dislike is not described as mutual, and
only the two strongest tension highlights accompany each pick.

A captain first favors the contestant with whom they have the strongest outgoing
relationship. Charisma breaks a tie, then average core skills, then a random
draw. Picks are final, and later decisions reflect any feelings changed by
earlier picks.

Being passed over can sting. Still-unpicked contestants lose a little affection
for both the captain and the person chosen: normally one point toward each.
Gentleness or maturity of at least 5 protects against that resentment; otherwise,
sensitivity of -5 or lower increases the loss to two points toward each.
The episode shows the strongest reactions rather than every small change.

The roster recap remains the main reference after every pick. Tension reactions
are indented notes beneath it, with clickable names. A later pick only introduces
reactions involving the newest teammate; it does not replay every earlier
disagreement on the team. These notes describe existing feelings rather than
automatically applying another relationship penalty.

#### Announcing the battles

Production announces three matchups and the songs the teams will perform.
Opposing arrangements have comparable difficulty.

Everyone reacts to someone on the opposing team they already feel strongly
about. Existing rivals or highly ambitious contestants may feel competitive
tension; others acknowledge their opponent with measured respect. These are
small, directional relationship changes, not automatic mutual hostility.

### Sequence 4: The first battle

The first team's rehearsal day receives full attention.

We see how the parts fit the contestants, who is comfortable, and who feels
overlooked or stretched. Two practice sessions test readiness. The captain's
leadership can bring everyone together or leave the group out of sync, changing
coordination and teammates' feelings.

The team votes for a center. Everyone votes for another teammate; the captain
can win. Visual appeal, stage presence, charisma, and existing relationships
influence each ballot. Tied totals favor charisma, then average core skill,
then a random draw.

The center receives **+1 temporary stage presence** for the performance, with
the effective skill capped at 5. This is not a permanent skill increase and does
not give every teammate an additional bonus.

#### A production decision

Each team's rehearsal includes its own player decision:
- **Support** adds 2 coordination and improves the featured pair's mutual
  relationship by 1.
- **Mediate** adds 1 coordination and improves their mutual relationship by 2.
- **Push** adds 3 coordination but reduces their mutual relationship by 3.

Coordination stays between -5 and +5. Each of the six teams gets its own
decision; choosing an approach for one team does not settle another's rehearsal.

A harmonious team discusses a rehearsal approach rather than suddenly having
an invented personal fight.

#### On stage

The first team performs. All five contestants receive individual evaluations
and grades; practice, delivery, temporary readiness, and team coordination matter.
The winner is not announced yet.

The opposing team watches from the wings. A strong performance may inspire
focused resolve or visible nerves, depending on the team's resilience. This
changes immediate stage readiness, not the practice that happened earlier.

We then flash back to the opposing team's rehearsal day, including its leader,
center ballot, and separate production decision. Returning to the present, the
second team performs.

### Sequence 5: The second battle

Repeat the same full structure for the next pair: the first team's rehearsal,
leadership, center vote, decision, and performance; the opponent's immediate
reaction; then its rehearsal flashback and performance.

Each team has its own actual strengths and tensions. Neither its rehearsals nor
its story is skipped because another team has already performed.

### Sequence 6: The third battle

The final pair receives the same coverage and decisions. By the end, all six
teams and all thirty contestants have performed.

Every team receives equal performance exposure, shared equally among its five
members. Screen time is not determined by how long the player leaves a scene open.

Audience voting closes quietly. There is no broadcast-package selection or
separate voting-results screen.

### Sequence 7: What the judges saw

Before any winner is announced, all three judges discuss every performance.
The episode reviews one battle at a time.

**John Stan** focuses on technical delivery. **Megan Ryder** considers stage
presence, potential, and uniqueness. **Michael Lion** evaluates visual appeal,
consistency, and leadership.

The existing battle result remains the main driver. The three judges have equal,
small influence: together, their evaluations can add at most **five team points**.
Their comments must match what actually happened, rather than inventing successes
or failures for drama.

After all three reviews, Mike returns to announce the three winners, one battle at
a time. The winning teams are safe. The losing teams are **provisionally
eliminated**, waiting for the final outcome. Nobody has permanently left yet.

Public votes do not choose the battle winners.

### Sequence 8: Three lifelines and a new ranking era

#### The saves

Mike reveals the twist: **three contestants from the losing teams can stay**.

First, the judges save the strongest individual battle performer among the
provisionally eliminated contestants.

Then, the audience saves the highest-voted remaining eligible contestant.

Finally, production and the label present three eligible nominees. The player
chooses the final save in a blocking decision.

The three saves must go to different people. Winning-team members do not consume
these lifelines.

#### Final goodbyes

Only after every save is locked do the twelve actual departures become final.
The losing teams receive paced goodbye scenes, with tears, relief, and reactions
grounded in real bonds.

A surviving contestant may resent the team that defeated an eliminated friend.
A friendship requires at least 60 outgoing affection toward the departing
contestant. Gentleness and maturity can soften the survivor's dislike; positive
sensitivity intensifies it, while negative sensitivity softens it.
The base penalty is ten points before personality adjustments. The change can
never become an affection gain, and the total loss toward any one opponent
cannot exceed fifteen points for the episode, even if multiple friends lost
to that opponent's team.

Saved friends do not trigger this backlash. Nobody blames themselves through
a self-relationship, and an eliminated contestant's fans only transfer after
the final outcome is known.

#### MVP and investigation

The eighteen survivors vote for a peer MVP. Nobody votes for themselves; the
honor adds no hidden ranking points.

The MVP gets a visible announcement before the player chooses one of three
contestants for a secret investigation. The investigation may uncover nothing.
If a secret would overturn the locked results, that consequence waits.

#### Mike's final announcement

Mike tells the contestants that the competition is changing:

**From the next episode onward, the contestants at the bottom of the ranking
will be eliminated.**

This does not overturn tonight's battles or saves. It establishes the stakes
of the episodes to come without announcing an unspecified future departure count.

#### The first complete survivor ranking

For the first time, Mike reveals the complete ranking of the **eighteen remaining
contestants**, from first through eighteenth. The earlier captain announcement
was only a partial glimpse.

The standings combine performance, public support, and production preference,
not just team victories or the latest judge comments. Contestant portraits appear
directly in the ranking, and their names still open their details.

After the audience has seen the ranking, the episode shows how it lands.
Everyone has a placement reaction: pride, relief, insecurity, frustration,
respect, or renewed determination.

Contestants may feel more supportive of, or resentful toward, the person one
place above them. Personality and existing affection decide the direction, with
small changes of no more than two points. First place has no higher-ranked target.
Only the strongest reactions need screen time.

These feelings do not recalculate the ranking or undo the saves, MVP, or
departures. They become part of the relationships carried into the next episode.

The episode ends with **eighteen survivors and twelve new departures**.
Episode 3 does not start automatically.

## Episode 3: Boot Camp

The eighteen Battles survivors head outdoors. Mike Philip hosts and introduces
Sargent Miller, who leads the assessment. The player starts the episode explicitly
and advances one recorded beat at a time; this is not a three-judge performance
panel or a permanent skill-training system.

### Before the episode

Retain opening-cast members still competing and replace only missing slots from
the strongest-core shortlist. Set six opening tastes using the highest- and
lowest-ranked survivors' specialties, with unique fallback options and Randomize.
Confirm these choices before the cold open. The usual +0.02 opening preference
applies once when the credits actually air.

### Sequence overview

| Sequence | What the audience watches                                                        |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1        | Empty beds and the survivors' opinions about the previous departures.            |
| 2        | The confirmed opening portraits.                                                 |
| 3        | Mike introduces Miller, three random groups, and blind pair selection.           |
| 4        | Blind ballots, Marathon Mic, all three pairs, and an individual winner.          |
| 5        | New blind ballots, Action Sequence, all three pairs, and a winner.               |
| 6        | Remaining pairs, Distraction Box, their performances, and a winner.              |
| 7        | All three groups attempt Sync Gauntlet; a winning group and individual standout. |
| 8        | Mike's surprise, relief, the peer MVP, and the updated continuing ranking.       |

### Sequence 1: Empty beds

The survivors return to their existing rooms, not new challenge-group dorms.
Missing roommates leave empty beds. Very extroverted contestants lose one
happiness per empty bed; very introverted contestants gain one. Moderate traits
have no automatic change. This bounded adjustment happens once, even after reload.
Every survivor voices an opinion grounded in personality and genuine outgoing
attachments. Roommates can agree, disagree, grieve, feel relieved, or find resolve;
the edit highlights meaningful responses without inventing friendships.

### Sequence 2: Opening

### Sequence 3: Outdoors

Mike introduces Sargent Miller. A saved shuffle forms three neutral groups of
six, separate from the surviving roommates. Introduce their full rosters and
actual first impressions. Small affection changes are directional; being grouped
together does not itself imply close friendship.

Miller explains that teammates must nominate pairs without knowing the next
exercise, followed by a full-group assessment. Nobody promises an elimination
count. Before the first two pair challenges, each of the six teammates chooses
two distinct eligible members; eligible self-votes are allowed. Used contestants
cannot be nominated again, although they still vote. Personality, maturity's
stronger/weaker balancing, and relationships inform ballots. Persisted random
keys break ties, not an undisclosed optimal-skill override. Lock all three groups'
pairs before each reveal.

### Sequence 4: Marathon Mic

Reveal the treadmill microphone challenge only after the blind nominations.
Each pair receives its own performance beat, exertion, reactions, and Miller's
assessment. Stamina and consistency dominate, with vocal/rap microphone delivery;
Dance contestants use their stronger vocal or rap skill. Discipline can help
pacing and resilience can support a low-stamina underdog. Sensitivity alone is
not failure: a recorded low roll is also needed for a panic/voice-crack failure.
Announce one individual winner across the six entrants.

### Sequence 5: Action Sequence

Repeat blind ballots using only unplayed teammates, then reveal an obstacle
course and synchronized vault/pose. Acrobatics matters most, but the pair's weaker
consistency genuinely affects synchronization. Show safe missteps, breakthroughs,
curiosity, goofing off, and actual reactions where supported by the rolls.
Ambitious overreach can be embarrassing, never a permanent injury. Charisma can
make a genuine failure charming without changing it into technical success.
After all three pairs perform, Miller announces an individual winner.

### Sequence 6: Distraction Box

The remaining two members of each group form its pair automatically; there is
no redundant ballot. Announce these pairs before revealing hand choreography
under sensory distractions. Consistency and learning drive the checks.
Resilience/maturity can protect timing; sensitivity plus low dice can disrupt it.
An actual mistake may earn an apology or sympathy, and a genuine camera moment
may earn coverage. Show every pair before naming the round's individual winner.

### Sequence 7: Sync Gauntlet

All eighteen perform in their groups, with saved staging. Each group gets full
attention: stamina, consistency, temporary fatigue, synchronization attempts,
Miller's assessments, and any real support or confrontation. Reset effects are
temporary corrections, not lasting skill growth or a route to higher raw quality.
At most five resets are allowed, then an assisted finish or safety stop.
High investment can sustain effort; low investment may produce checking out
after repeated resets. Show safe exhaustion or rest, not dangerous collapse.

Announce the winning group and one individual standout eligible from all
eighteen, including members outside the winning group. A previous winner can
win again. Winner benefits are applied internally, never explained as point
bonuses in the story.

Only now does Mike announce the surprise: all eighteen remain. Let the

The new 1-18 continuing ranking combines public voting and episode performance:
50% pair individual score, 30% final individual score, and 20% final group score,
with winner benefits applied internally after ordinary ranking resolution. Rewards improve
prospects, not guarantee a fixed placement rise. Show meaningful actual movement
from Episode 2. Clear only this episode's temporary fatigue, preserving starting
condition and practice. End without a secret investigation or Episode 4.

### Blind teams and genuine comedy

Teams are one saved random 4/4/4 draw, never skill- or affection-balanced.
Extroverts can hype their real teams; a genuine high/low ambition clash can
cause bounded reciprocal friction, not blanket hostility among strangers.

Physical variety relies on stamina, acrobatics and charisma. Exaggerated faces
and harmless gags can earn actual coverage; discipline alone is not a penalty.
An inflexible contestant with weak saved charisma can look stiff.

Interviews rely on charisma, sincerity and maturity. Thoughtful answers can
endear contestants to Roxana. A calculated answer is called fake only when
the actual low-roll/trait conditions support a failure. Sensitive contestants
can recover charmingly or fumble, rather than failing automatically. Prompts
use public information, never an automatic hidden-secret revelation.

Improv relies on charisma, curiosity and extroversion, with individual ideas,
group structure and ensemble chemistry. Introverted members can succeed
through creativity and delivery. Resilience can rescue a genuinely failed
joke without pretending its original setup landed or rerolling the performance.
Without anyone at leadership 3 or above, team organization suffers even when
an individual joke succeeds.

One improv leader is elected per team using normal leadership/style/affection
priorities. There is no center election or new music rehearsal. Support,
Mediate and Push are offered only for a recorded preparation disagreement;
they affect bounded episode coordination and the involved pair's affection.
All production decisions block advancement until saved.

checks. Personality contributions normalize directly to 0-100. Physical uses
40% stamina/35% acrobatics/25% charisma; interviews 50% charisma/25% sincerity/
25% maturity; improv 40% charisma/35% curiosity/25% extroversion. Actual personal
event modifiers total at most -10..+10.

Each act's final result is **70% personal and 30% team**. Physical/interview team
results are true means of four completed answers, never precomputed unseen
contributions. Improv also includes bounded affection cohesion, preparation
coordination and any actual organizational penalty.

The overall variety result and all four judges contribute **20% each**:

and ensemble results, Roxana sustained charisma and the weakest personal act.
Roxana joins rather than replaces a judge; her assessment is not a second flat
guest bonus. No primary-track, music, growth or icebreaker score bonus applies.

All twelve privately choose any other contestant for comic MVP, not just a
teammate, using actual comic evidence and existing affection/personality rules.
Audience and cumulative ranking resolve once using the existing formula.
